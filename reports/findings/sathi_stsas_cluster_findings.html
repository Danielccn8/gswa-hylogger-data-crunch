sTSAS Cluster Findings

A scanner measured the minerals down 20 drill holes across Western Australia. This page sorts that core into rock types, and shows which holes are made of similar rock — without anyone labelling it by hand.

01

What kinds of rock are down there?

The computer was given one row per metre of core, listing how much of each of 44 minerals it contained — and nothing else. No hole names, no depths, no locations. It sorted those metres into groups of similar mineral mixtures. Each group below is named after whatever it turned out to be made of.

The 8 rock types, and what each is made of

The numbers are proportions of the mixture. Chlorite 0.61 means that in an average metre of this group, chlorite makes up 61% of the minerals present.

In plain terms

Seven of the eight groups are dominated by a single mineral, so they read like familiar rock types — chlorite alteration, amphibolite, white micas, carbonate, ultramafic. Group 2 is different: nothing dominates it. It is genuinely mixed rock, and it covers 2,082 m — more than a fifth of all the core measured.

02

Which drill holes are alike?

Once every metre has a rock type, a hole can be described by its recipe: what share of it is each type. A hole that is 70% chlorite and 10% carbonate has a different recipe from one that is 80% dolomite — and recipes can be compared even when the holes are different lengths.

    In plain terms

    The score is just how similar two recipes are, from 0% (nothing in common) to 100% (identical). Nothing about a hole's name, location, depth or length is used — only what it is made of.

    A check that it works

    16MYD0047 and 16MYD0052 came out as the 2nd most similar pair of all 190, at 84.1%. Their names differ by one character — they are almost certainly from the same drilling programme. The same happened with PND003, PND004 and PND005. The method was never told any of this. It matched them purely on minerals, which is good evidence it is finding real geology rather than noise.

    The closest match for each hole

    “Mutual” means the two holes each pick the other as their closest match — a stronger result than a one-way match. Nine of the twenty pairs are mutual.

    03

    Is the computer telling us anything we didn't know?

    There is a much simpler way to sort core: just label each metre by whichever mineral there is most of. No computer needed. If the grouping is only repeating that, then the work has produced nothing new — so it has to be checked, not assumed.

    Grouping by mineral mixture vs. just taking the biggest mineral

    The bar shows how closely the two methods agree. A full bar would mean they give identical answers, and the modelling adds nothing.

    In plain terms

    Where a metre of core is obviously “mostly chlorite”, the grouping agrees with the simple shortcut almost completely — it adds little there. But across the 2,990 m of genuinely mixed rock, where no single mineral stands out, the two disagree a lot. That mixed rock is where this work earns its place — the shortcut cannot describe it, and it is nearly a third of all the core.

    04

    Why eight groups and not five, or twenty?

    Nobody chose eight. The computer has to be told how many groups to make, so it was asked to try every number from 2 to 15, and each attempt was scored on how cleanly the groups stood apart. A fixed rule then picked the winner — which means a new set of drill holes gets its own answer without anyone deciding by hand.

    How cleanly the groups separate, for each number of groups

    —

    In plain terms

    The line climbs steeply up to 8 groups — each extra group is finding something real. After that it goes flat, so splitting further just cuts the same rock into smaller pieces. The rule is “take the simplest answer that is as good as the best one”, which lands on 8. It also reports that 8, 11, 12 and 13 all score about the same, rather than pretending 8 is certain.

    05

    How the raw scan data was prepared

    None of the above would have worked on the raw data. The scanner reads a spot every 7 mm — about 116 readings for every metre of core. A spot that small usually catches just one or two minerals, so the raw rows describe individual grains, not rock.

    What reached the model

    Rows removed at each stage, and why. Every exclusion is recorded in its own file — nothing was dropped silently.

    Averaging each metre of core

    The 116 readings in each metre were averaged into one row. No measurement was thrown away — all 1,018,271 readings still count towards the metre they fall in.

    Measureraw 7 mm rows1 m rows
    Why this step mattered

    In the raw data, 359,217 rows contained a single mineral and nothing else, and 381,620 rows were exact copies of another row. A million rows sounded like a huge dataset, but it was really the same rock measured over and over. The honest figure is about 9,600 metres of core — and after averaging, a typical row describes a mixture of roughly 8 minerals instead of 1 or 2.

    06

    The three terms used above

    Separation score (silhouette)
    How cleanly the groups stand apart, from 0 to 1. Around 0 means there are no real groups; 1 would mean perfectly separate ones. This work scores 0.41 — moderate, honest structure, which is normal for natural geology and not something to dress up.
    Agreement score (Adjusted Rand Index)
    Whether two different methods put the same metres into the same groups, from 0 to 1. It ignores what the groups are called, so it does not matter that one says “group 3” and the other says “chlorite”.
    Recipe (cluster fingerprint)
    The share of a hole's core that falls in each rock type. Because every row covers exactly one metre, the share of rows is also the share of metres — so a 978 m hole and a 196 m hole can be compared fairly.
